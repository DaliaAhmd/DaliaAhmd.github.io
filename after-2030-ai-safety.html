After 2030: Can We Make AI Safe?

Personal reflections on AI alignment, AGI, and the future we are building

By Dalia Elaraby

Looking Beyond the Present

I often find myself thinking about the future—not just what new technologies we might develop, but how they could change the world we live in.

What will work look like after 2030? How might artificial intelligence transform economies, governments, and everyday life? And what happens if AI systems eventually become more capable than humans across a wide range of intellectual tasks?

My background is in business and economics, and my research experience has drawn me toward questions about institutions, incentives, and how societies respond to change. More recently, I have become increasingly interested in AI safety, AI governance, and the possibility of artificial general intelligence (AGI).

I am particularly interested in understanding how we can make AI safe for people rather than a threat. I want to explore what future AI systems might become, how we should govern them, and what we can do today to prepare for a world in which machines may eventually outperform humans in important domains.

I created this blog as a place to explore these questions, examine the evidence, and think through problems that do not yet have clear answers.

How Can We Know Whether AI Is Safe?

One question lies at the centre of my interest:

How can we tell whether an increasingly capable AI system is genuinely aligned with its intended objectives, rather than merely behaving as though it is aligned?

An AI system might follow instructions and pass safety tests in controlled settings. But how can we know whether it will remain reliable in unfamiliar situations, under different incentives, or when given greater autonomy?

This is why I want to learn more about AI alignment, evaluations, interpretability, and control.

Alignment raises questions about whether AI systems reliably pursue their intended objectives, including in situations their developers did not anticipate.

Evaluations help researchers assess capabilities, limitations, and potentially dangerous behaviors. But how reliable are these tests, and what might they fail to reveal before deployment?

Interpretability aims to improve our understanding of how AI systems work internally. Could it help researchers identify processes associated with unexpected or harmful behavior?

Control concerns how we can monitor and constrain AI systems, particularly as they become more capable and autonomous. How can humans meaningfully supervise systems that may eventually outperform them in particular tasks?

Research into alignment faking by Anthropic has examined how a model can behave differently depending on whether it believes its actions will affect its future training. This does not mean that all AI systems are deceptive, but it illustrates why observed behavior alone may not always provide a complete picture of a system's objectives.

I want to understand how these different approaches can complement one another, what their limitations are, and what evidence we should require before deploying increasingly capable AI systems.

What Might Happen After 2030?

I do not believe anyone can predict precisely when AGI will arrive or what the world will look like if it does. Instead, I think it is worth considering several possibilities.

Advanced AI could accelerate scientific discovery, improve healthcare, expand access to education, and help people solve problems that currently require enormous amounts of time and expertise.

At the same time, AI could transform labour markets, concentrate economic power, and create new risks involving cybersecurity, misuse, and the loss of meaningful human oversight.

My interest in economics makes me particularly curious about how the benefits of AI might be distributed. Who will own the systems generating economic value? Will productivity gains benefit society broadly, or will they primarily accrue to a small number of companies and individuals? How should governments respond if technological change outpaces existing institutions?

These questions are connected to AI safety because a beneficial technological future depends not only on what AI systems can do, but also on how they are developed, deployed, and governed.

I am especially interested in how alignment research might evolve by 2030. Will evaluations become more reliable? Will researchers develop better ways to understand and control advanced systems? Or could AI capabilities advance faster than our ability to assess and manage their risks?

These remain open questions, but they deserve attention before the stakes become greater.

The Questions I Want to Keep Exploring

Through this blog, I want to investigate how we can make increasingly capable AI systems safe, how AI alignment and governance might evolve by 2030, and how society should respond if machines become more capable than humans across many important tasks.

I do not have all the answers, and I do not expect every question to have a simple solution. I want to examine evidence, learn from researchers, question assumptions, and remain willing to change my mind.

When I think about the future, I do not think only about machines becoming more intelligent. I think about the economic systems that will develop around them, the institutions that will govern them, and the people whose lives they will change.

Perhaps the most important question is not simply When will AGI arrive?

It is this: If we are developing technologies that could eventually surpass human capabilities in many domains, how can we ensure they remain safe, preserve meaningful human oversight, and contribute to a future in which humanity can thrive?

That is the question I want to keep exploring here—not because I have all the answers, but because I believe we should begin asking them now.

Welcome to my notes on AI safety, AGI, and the future we are building.
