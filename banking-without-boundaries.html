Banking Without Boundaries

How the repeal of Glass–Steagall and branching deregulation reshaped American banking

By Dalia Ahmed

America once feared big banks. The country’s financial history, born from populist distrust of concentrated power, built walls to keep lenders small and local. The McFadden Act of 1927 cemented this approach, barring national banks from opening branches across state lines. The goal was to preserve community banking and competition; the result was a fragmented system of thousands of small, fragile institutions.

The Great Depression shattered faith in that model. When thousands of banks failed, lawmakers rushed to restore confidence through regulation. The Banking Act of 1933—better known for its Glass-Steagall provisions—erected another wall: this time between commercial and investment banking. Lenders could take deposits and make loans, or they could underwrite securities, but not both. The Bank Holding Company Act of 1956 reinforced those boundaries, forbidding banks from mixing finance with insurance. Stability returned, but at the cost of flexibility.

By the 1970s, those barriers looked increasingly outdated. American banks were hemmed in by geography and regulation while foreign rivals expanded abroad. State-level deregulation began quietly in the 1980s, as the Federal Reserve and the FDIC eased their interpretations of Glass-Steagall. The logic of separation crumbled not by revolution but erosion. Banks found loopholes; regulators turned a blind eye.

The real breakthrough came in 1994, when the Riegle-Neal Act allowed interstate branching for the first time in decades. Suddenly, banks could operate across the country, achieving economies of scale once unimaginable. Consolidation followed: local lenders merged into regional players, and regional players into national behemoths.

The coup de grâce arrived in 1998, when Citicorp merged with Travelers Group, combining banking and insurance under one roof—technically illegal at the time, but politically unstoppable. A year later, Congress made it official. The Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act of 1999 repealed the Glass-Steagall barriers, legalizing the universal bank model America had spent most of the century avoiding.

Those reforms unleashed a wave of financial innovation—and risk. Banks that once could not cross state lines or mix business lines became sprawling conglomerates with global reach. The logic of competition and efficiency triumphed over caution. Less than a decade later, the financial crisis of 2008 revealed the cost of that freedom.

America’s banking system today is the product of that long tug-of-war between fragmentation and integration. Each generation built walls, only for the next to tear them down. The question, as policymakers again confront the power of “too big to fail” institutions, is whether new boundaries will hold any longer than the old ones did.
